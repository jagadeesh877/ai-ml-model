The Lion and the Clever Mouse
One warm afternoon, in a vast golden savanna bordered by green acacia trees, a mighty lion was resting in the shade of a great rock. His golden mane was thick and soft, and his great paws were stretched out upon the warm sand.
A playful little field mouse named Pip was scurrying through the tall grass, searching for seeds. Not noticing the sleeping beast, Pip ran straight across the lion's broad nose.
With a deep, rumbling growl, the lion awoke. He raised his enormous paw and trapped the tiny mouse beneath his sharp claws.
"How dare you wake the king of the beasts!" roared the lion, opening his great jaws. "You shall pay for your carelessness with your life!"
The little mouse trembled from head to tail, but he gathered his courage and spoke in a clear, squeaky voice: "O mighty king, forgive me! I did not mean to disturb your royal slumber. If you spare my life today, I promise that one day I will repay your kindness!"
The lion looked down at the tiny creature and laughed so hard that the dry grass shook. "You? A tiny mouse help the king of beasts? That is the funniest joke I have ever heard!" But because the lion was amused and not truly hungry, he lifted his paw and let the little mouse run away into the grass.
Several weeks later, while prowling through the forest, the lion stepped into a hunter's trap. A thick net of strong ropes fell over him, pulling tight around his body. The lion roared in fury and struggled with all his strength, but the ropes only tightened further.
Hearing the familiar roar echoing across the valley, little Pip ran to the scene. He saw the great lion bound and helpless against the trunk of an acacia tree.
"Do not despair, noble friend!" cried Pip. "I will set you free!"
Pip immediately went to work with his sharp teeth. He gnawed steadily through the thick ropes, biting through strand after strand with tireless patience. Within a short time, the net gave way, and the great lion stepped out, free once more.
The lion bowed his great head to the tiny mouse and said softly, "You laughed at my laughter, little friend, and taught me a lesson I will never forget. No act of kindness is ever wasted, and even the smallest creature can help the greatest of all."

The Wind and the Radiant Sun
A dispute once arose between the North Wind and the Sun as to which of them was the stronger of the two.
"Look at my power!" howled the North Wind, blowing a freezing gale across the plains. "I can tear up the tallest pine trees by their roots, blow the roofs off cottages, and turn the gentle rivers into solid sheets of ice!"
The Sun smiled warmly through the blue sky. "True strength does not lie in loud destruction, friend Wind. True strength lies in gentle persuasion and quiet persistence."
The Wind laughed haughtily. "Then let us test our powers on a traveler! Look down at that man walking along the dusty road, wearing a heavy wool cloak. Whichever of us can make him take off his cloak first shall be declared the victor."
"I accept your challenge," replied the Sun. "You may make the first attempt, while I step behind a white cloud."
The North Wind took a deep breath, puffed out his cheeks, and blew with all his fury. A howling blizzard swept down upon the traveler. Frost covered the grass, and dead leaves whirled in the air.
The traveler pulled his wool cloak tightly around his shoulders. The Wind blew harder and colder, tearing at the traveler's hem. But the harder the Wind blew, the more firmly the traveler wrapped his cloak around himself, clutching the collar with both hands. Finally, exhausted and out of breath, the Wind gave up in defeat.
Then the Sun stepped out from behind the cloud and shone down with gentle, radiant warmth. The cold frost melted from the meadows, the birds began to sing in the green branches, and the air turned pleasant and sweet.
The traveler unbuttoned the top clasp of his cloak as the warmth spread over his back. The Sun shone brighter and warmer, filling the valley with golden light. The traveler paused, wiped the perspiration from his brow, and gladly took off his heavy cloak, carrying it over his arm as he walked happily down the road.
The Wind bowed his head in silence. Gentleness and kind warmth had succeeded where brute force and angry fury had failed.

The Two Friends and the Forest Bear
Two young travelers, Daniel and Matthew, were journeying together through a wild and lonely forest. As they walked along the stony trail, they promised each other that in case of any danger, they would stand together and protect one another like true brothers.
They had not walked much farther when the bushes suddenly parted, and an enormous brown bear charged onto the path, growling fiercely.
Daniel, who was swift of foot, instantly forgot his promise. He dashed to the nearest tall tree, caught hold of a low branch, and scrambled high into the safety of the green leaves, leaving his companion alone on the ground.
Matthew knew that he was not fast enough to outrun the bear, and he could not climb the tree in time. Remembering an old country tale that a bear will not touch a dead body, Matthew threw himself flat upon the ground, pressed his face into the dry leaves, and held his breath as still as a stone.
The great bear lumbered over to the fallen traveler. The beast leaned down, sniffing Matthew's ears, neck, and hair. Matthew lay perfectly motionless, his eyes tightly shut, not daring to move a single muscle. The bear nudged his shoulder with its wet nose, smelled the cold breath, and concluded that the man was lifeless. With a low snort, the bear turned away and vanished into the thick undergrowth.
When the danger had passed, Daniel climbed down from the tree with a sheepish grin. "Well, Matthew," he asked, "that was a close call! Tell me, what did that old bear whisper into your ear as you lay on the ground?"
Matthew brushed the dry leaves from his coat, looked his companion straight in the eyes, and replied quietly:
"The bear gave me a very good piece of advice: Never travel through life with a friend who deserts you the moment trouble arrives."

The Singing Nightingale of the Golden Palace
In the grand capital of an ancient empire, the Emperor lived in a magnificent palace surrounded by gardens filled with porcelain bells that chimed in the breeze. Beyond the gardens stretched a deep, tranquil forest where a little gray nightingale lived in a tall willow tree.
Every evening, the nightingale sang with such wondrous sweetness that fishermen paused in their boats, tears in their eyes, listening to the pure melody.
When travelers wrote books praising the nightingale's song, the Emperor read the reports and commanded, "Bring this wonderful bird to my court! If the entire world knows of its song, I must hear it myself."
The royal servants searched the forest and found the modest gray bird sitting on a willow bough. They invited the nightingale to the palace, where hundreds of lamps illuminated the golden halls.
The nightingale sang, and its song was so pure, so tender, and so full of longing that the Emperor himself wept with emotion. "You shall live in the palace for ever," said the Emperor, "and have twelve attendants to care for you."
A few months later, the King of a neighboring land sent the Emperor a gift: an artificial bird made of gold and diamonds. When wound with a silver key, the mechanical bird sang a lively waltz, wagging its tail to the rhythm. The court was fascinated by the glittering toy, and while they listened to the mechanical melody, the real nightingale quietly flew back to its beloved green forest.
Years passed. One winter, the mechanical bird broke its clockwork springs and fell silent. Soon after, the Emperor fell gravely ill. He lay pale and cold in his royal bed, and his courtiers deserted the throne room, believing the end was near.
Suddenly, from the open window, a sweet and heavenly melody drifted into the quiet chamber. The little gray nightingale had returned. It perched upon a flowering branch outside and poured its heart into song, singing of the peaceful churchyard, the morning dew, and the blooming roses.
As the nightingale sang, the color returned to the Emperor's pale cheeks, warmth flowed through his limbs, and his heart beat strong and steady once more.
"Thank you, little singer of the forest," whispered the Emperor with tears of joy. "How can I ever repay your gift of life?"
The nightingale tilted its head and replied, "You have already rewarded me, noble Emperor. You wept the first time you heard me sing, and those tears of true feeling are the jewels that no golden cage can buy. I will return every evening at twilight to sing to you of the happy and the sad, of the good and the evil that is hidden around your kingdom. Only promise me that you will remain kind and true to all your people."
The Emperor promised, and under the inspiration of the forest bird's honest song, he ruled his land with wisdom, mercy, and compassion for many long and prosperous years.

The Honest Farmer and the Golden Harvest
In a sun-drenched valley between two green hills lived an honest farmer named Gregory. Gregory worked from sunrise to sunset in his fields, planting wheat, barley, and sweet corn. Though he had little money and lived in a simple stone cottage with a thatched roof, his home was always filled with warmth, hospitality, and gratitude.
One summer, a terrible drought struck the kingdom. The rains failed to fall, the creeks dried to muddy trickles, and the crops across the valley withered in the scorching sun.
While walking across his parched field, Gregory found an injured white dove with a broken wing lying helpless beneath an old scarecrow. Gregory gently gathered the bird into his calloused hands, brought it to his cottage, set the broken wing with soft wooden splints, and fed it the last of his grain and fresh water from his deep well.
When the dove was healed and strong enough to fly, it did not immediately take to the sky. Instead, it circled three times above Gregory's head and dropped a small, glittering seed into his palm.
"Plant this seed in the center of your dry field," whispered the gentle dove, "and let your generous heart guide the harvest."
Gregory did as he was told. He planted the seed in the dry earth and watered it with the last cup of water from his well.
The next morning, Gregory woke to a miraculous sight. Out of the dry ground had grown a tall, golden stalk of wheat, bearing hundreds of heavy ears that shimmered like pure sunlight. When Gregory harvested the grain, he found that the ears did not exhaust themselves: whenever one ear was plucked, two more grew in its place!
Instead of selling the miraculous grain for a high price, Gregory invited all the hungry families of the valley to his farm. He distributed bags of golden grain to every cottage, taught them how to plant and nurture the new seed, and ensured that no child in the valley went hungry during the drought.
When the autumn rains finally returned, the valley was more fertile than ever before. The people honored Gregory as their beloved elder, having learned that true wealth is measured not by what you keep, but by what you share.
